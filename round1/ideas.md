## A. ASHA home visit, recorded by voice (Healthcare)

The task: Recording a home visit. After each visit to a pregnant woman or a newborn, an ASHA worker writes what she found into paper registers and later types the same details into her state's health app, or hands them to the ANM to enter.

Who does it and what is hard today: India's ASHA workers, about ten lakh women who visit homes on foot in villages and urban slums. During a visit her hands are full with the BP machine, the weighing scale and the mother's MCP card. The forms are in English or Hindi columns, while she thinks and speaks in Kannada, Odia, Bhojpuri or her own dialect. So she fills them in at night from memory, writes everything twice, and entries get delayed or skipped. A danger sign, like high BP and swelling in the eighth month, can reach the ANM days late.

The voice-first steps:
1. Still in the home, she says "New visit, Lakshmi, Kalenahalli". The app finds Lakshmi's record and replies in Kannada: "Lakshmi, 32 weeks pregnant, last visit 12 November. Is that right?" She says "Yes."
2. She describes the visit in her own words, mixing languages as she normally does: "BP 150 by 100, weight 58 kilo, feet swollen for two days, she is taking her iron tablets."
3. The app fills in the visit form and asks only about what is missing: "Any headache or blurred vision?" She says "Headache, yes."
4. It reads the record back to her. Because BP is high with swelling and headache, the fixed protocol (not the AI) says she should be referred to the PHC today, so it asks "Shall I alert the ANM?" She says "Yes, alert."
5. It saves the visit, keeps it on the phone if there is no network and sends it later, alerts the ANM, and confirms aloud: "Saved. ANM Shobha has been told."

Why voice works better: She can record the visit while it happens, with her hands and eyes free for the mother and the baby. She speaks once, in her own language, instead of writing in one script and typing in another. Records become complete and on time, and a danger sign reaches the ANM the same day instead of at the next meeting. It works for ASHA workers who find typing on a phone hard.

## B. Kirana order from a voice note to a bill (Small business)

The task: Taking a home-delivery order at a neighbourhood kirana store and making the bill. Regular customers send a WhatsApp voice note or call: "Bhaiya, do kilo atta, ek Fortune tel, aur Maggi ke chaar packet." The owner listens, often twice, writes a chit, looks up prices, totals them on a calculator, then types the amount into a UPI request or the credit book.

Who does it and what is hard today: Owners and helpers of India's neighbourhood kirana stores, usually while serving customers at the counter. Brand names and quantities said in Hindi, Kannada or a mix get misheard or missed. Billing apps expect typed English item names. Every order is handled twice, by ear and then by hand, and mistakes mean a second delivery or an argument about the bill.

The voice-first steps:
1. The owner shares the customer's voice note to the app, or just says "New order, Sharma ji".
2. The app understands the order in the customer's language, matches it to the shop's own items and prices, and reads it back: "Two kilo Aashirvaad atta 110, one litre Fortune oil 155, four Maggi 56. Total 321."
3. The owner corrects it by speaking: "Atta is Pillsbury."
4. The app asks only about what is unclear: "Small Maggi or the big pack?" The owner says "Small."
5. The owner says "Send". The customer gets the bill and a UPI payment link on WhatsApp, the helper gets a packing list, and the owner later says "Sharma ji paid" or "Put it on credit."

Why voice works better: Orders already arrive as speech, so nothing has to be retyped. The owner's hands stay free at the counter, item names never need to be typed in English, and the read-back catches missed items before packing, not after delivery.

## C. Reading check for Class 1 to 3, by voice (Education)

The task: Running a reading check for each child in Class 1 to 3 and entering the score. Under NIPUN Bharat, a teacher listens to each child read a short passage aloud, counts the words read correctly in one minute, and types the score into the assessment app or register.

Who does it and what is hard today: Primary teachers in government schools, often with 30 to 40 children across more than one class. Timing a child on a phone, counting mistakes while listening, and typing each score takes a whole period or more. So checks happen rarely, and children who are falling behind are noticed late.

The voice-first steps:
1. The teacher says "Class 2 reading check, Kannada passage 3." The passage appears on the phone for the child.
2. She says the child's roll number: "Roll 14, Anitha."
3. Anitha reads aloud. The app listens and marks each word as read correctly, skipped or changed.
4. It says the result: "Anitha, 38 words correct per minute, struggled with joined letters." The teacher says "Next" to move on, or "Again" if the room was noisy.
5. At the end she says "Read me the class summary", and the app lists the children below the grade's target and saves the scores to the school's record.

Why voice works better: Reading aloud is the test itself, so voice is the natural way to record it. The teacher watches the child instead of a stopwatch and a keyboard, every child is scored the same way, and checks can happen every month instead of once a term.
